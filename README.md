## Dataset Title:
- U.S. Food Imports Dataset
## Describing your Dataset and What Data it Contains:
This dataset provides a comprehensive record of food products imported into the United States over multiple decades. It captures essential trade details such as country of origin, product type, volume/value of goods, and year of import.

Each row represents a unique import entry, allowing for granular insights into what commodities are being imported, from where, in what quantities, and at what dollar value. This supports trend analysis, supply chain monitoring, and international trade evaluation.

Key Variables:
- Country: Nation exporting food to the U.S.

- Commodity: Specific food item (Coffee, Wheat)

- Category: General food group (Grains, Fruits)

- UOM: Unit of measurement (KG, LITERS)

- YearNum: Calendar year of import

- FoodValue: Dollar value of the imported item

## Analysis and Results:
Analysis of Results for Question 1:

Mexico experienced the most dramatic increase in food export value to the U.S., significantly outpacing all other countries by 2024. Key contributing factors:

- Low transportation costs and shorter supply chains

- Reduced tariffs and trade barriers

- Rising U.S. demand for fresh produce like fruits and vegetables

Canada remained a consistently strong trade partner throughout the timeline. Key contributing factors:

- Shared climate zones that support similar agricultural cycles

- Geographic proximity and the largest shared international border with the U.S.

- Reduced tariffs due to trade agreements like USMCA

China’s trend was relatively flat and even showed a decline starting around 2018. Key contributing factors:

- Increased political tensions and economic sanctions

- Introduction or reintroduction of tariffs in key sectors

Analysis of Results for Question 2:

Key Observations:
- The majority of the top exporters to the United States were located in Central and South America, regions known for their agricultural output and climate compatibility with key crops.

- Brazil maintained its position as a leading exporter to the U.S. throughout the 25-year span, particularly in the coffee, tea, and spice industry. This indicates the strength and consistency of Brazil's production and trade infrastructure.

Why This Is Interesting and Important:
- It’s essential for the U.S. to understand which countries consistently serve as major trading partners, especially in specific food categories.

- Recognizing these long-term trade relationships enables the U.S. to prioritize stable partnerships, negotiate better trade agreements, and ensure supply chain resilience.

Why did it Change?
- India, historically a major producer of coffee and spices, initially held a strong share of exports. However, Vietnam emerged as a more cost-effective supplier, offering faster production and cheaper exports, leading to India’s decline in this category.

- Canada operated largely as a re-exporter of coffee, tea, and spices. Over time, the U.S. began reducing reliance on such intermediaries in favor of direct importers, cutting middleman costs.

- Switzerland, while not an agricultural producer itself, became a hub for high-end coffee processing and exports, primarily due to companies like Nestlé. This helped it briefly appear in the top five, despite limited in-house production.


## Overall Analysis:
- Average import value is growing and suggests that pricing, volume, or dependency are increasing.
- America appears to be acquiring more imports from Central and South America than the other continents of the world. 
- Countries like Mexico, Chile, Vietnam are showing signs of rapid economic growth, indicating strong agricultural export capacity, increased demand from those countries and even better trade agreements.
